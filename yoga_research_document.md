## 1. What is Yoga? Origin and Global Practice

### Definition

Yoga is a group of physical, mental, and spiritual practices that originated in ancient India. The word "yoga" derives from the Sanskrit *योग* (yōga), meaning **"yoke" or "union"** — symbolizing the union of body, mind, and spirit. At its core, yoga is a discipline that seeks to harmonize the individual self with universal consciousness, and in modern practice, it is widely used as a system of physical exercise, breathing techniques, and meditation.

Yoga encompasses a broad range of practices, from vigorous physical postures (asanas) to controlled breathwork (pranayama), focused meditation (dhyana), and ethical philosophy. The ultimate aim, rooted in its ancient origins, is to attain liberation (*moksha*) — freedom from suffering and the cycle of rebirth. In contemporary Western practice, yoga is often approached primarily as a method for improving physical fitness, flexibility, stress reduction, and overall well-being.

### Origins

Yoga's roots stretch back more than **5,000 years**, making it one of the oldest known systems of personal development in the world.

**Pre-Vedic Period (3000–1500 BCE):** Some of the earliest archaeological evidence of yogic practices comes from the **Indus Valley Civilization**, which flourished in what is now modern-day Pakistan and northwestern India. Seals and artifacts discovered from sites such as Mohenjo-Daro depict figures seated in postures resembling meditative or yogic positions, suggesting that proto-yogic practices existed even before formal documentation.

**Vedic Period (1500–500 BCE):** The first textual reference to yoga appears in the **Rig Veda**, one of the oldest known scriptures in the world. The Vedic priests, known as Brahmins, used yoga as part of their ritual practices. The **Upanishads**, a collection of ancient philosophical texts, further developed yogic philosophy, including the concept of *Brahman* (universal consciousness) and *Atman* (the individual soul).

**Pre-Classical Period (500 BCE–200 CE):** During this era, the **Bhagavad Gita** — one of the most celebrated Hindu scriptures — was composed. It described three paths of yoga: Karma Yoga (the path of action), Jnana Yoga (the path of wisdom), and Bhakti Yoga (the path of devotion). Buddhism and Jainism, which emerged during this period, also incorporated yogic meditative practices.

**Classical Period (~200 BCE):** The **Yoga Sutras of Patanjali**, compiled around 200 BCE, represent the first systematic presentation of yoga philosophy. Patanjali outlined the "Eight Limbs of Yoga" (Ashtanga) — a comprehensive guide encompassing ethical principles, physical postures, breath control, sensory withdrawal, concentration, meditation, and enlightenment. This work remains the foundational text of classical yoga philosophy.

**Post-Classical Period (500–1500 CE):** Yoga teachers rejected the teachings of the ancient Vedas and developed new practices to rejuvenate the body. This led to the creation of **Tantra Yoga** and, eventually, **Hatha Yoga**. The **Hatha Yoga Pradipika**, written by Swami Swatmarama in the 15th century, became the cornerstone of Hatha Yoga, emphasizing physical purification techniques (shatkarmas), postures (asanas), and breath control (pranayama).

**Modern Period (Late 19th Century–Present):** The introduction of yoga to the Western world is credited primarily to **Swami Vivekananda**, who delivered his historic speech at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago in 1893, introducing yoga's philosophical dimensions to a global audience. In the 1920s, **Paramahansa Yogananda** further spread yoga-meditation teachings in the United States. In the 20th century, **Tirumalai Krishnamacharya** — often called "the father of modern yoga" — trained a generation of influential teachers including B.K.S. Iyengar, K. Pattabhi Jois, and T.K.V. Desikachar, whose work shaped the various styles of yoga practiced today.

### Major Styles of Yoga

| Style | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Hatha Yoga** | Foundational style focusing on physical postures and breathing; suitable for beginners |
| **Vinyasa Yoga** | Dynamic, flow-based style linking breath with movement |
| **Ashtanga Yoga** | A rigorous, set sequence of poses practiced in a specific order |
| **Iyengar Yoga** | Focuses on precision, alignment, and use of props |
| **Kundalini Yoga** | Combines postures, breathwork, chanting, and meditation to awaken energy |
| **Bikram/Hot Yoga** | 26 poses practiced in a heated room (~40°C/104°F) |
| **Yin Yoga** | Slow-paced style holding poses for several minutes to target connective tissue |
| **Restorative Yoga** | Uses props to support the body in passive poses, promoting deep relaxation |
| **Power Yoga** | A vigorous, fitness-based approach derived from Ashtanga |

### Where Yoga Is Practiced Today

Yoga has grown from an ancient Indian spiritual practice into a **global wellness phenomenon**. Today, it is practiced on every continent and is recognized by major international institutions.

**India** remains the heartland of yoga, where it is cherished both as a spiritual discipline and a health practice. The government of India actively promotes yoga internationally — a fact reflected in the **United Nations General Assembly's declaration of June 21 as the International Day of Yoga**, celebrated worldwide since 2015. In December 2016, yoga was inscribed on **UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity**.

**North America** represents the largest commercial yoga market. The United States alone has seen remarkable growth, with tens of millions of Americans practicing yoga regularly. The yoga industry — encompassing classes, clothing (such as yoga pants), mats, accessories, books, and retreat tourism — has grown into a **multi-billion-dollar global business**.

**Europe**, particularly the United Kingdom, Germany, France, and Scandinavia, has seen significant adoption of yoga as a mainstream fitness and wellness activity.

**Asia-Pacific** markets, including Australia, Japan, South Korea, and China, have also seen substantial growth in yoga participation, both as physical exercise and as mindfulness practice.

Yoga is now practiced even in many **Middle Eastern countries**, including Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, and Iran, which have begun acknowledging the holistic health benefits of the practice. India's **Medical Value Travel (MVT)** market — which includes yoga and wellness tourism — was estimated at $6 billion in 2022 and was projected to grow to $13 billion by 2026.

## 2. Health Benefits of Yoga

Decades of scientific research have documented a broad range of physical and mental health benefits from regular yoga practice. While more rigorous studies are still needed in some areas, the existing evidence strongly supports yoga as an effective complementary health practice.

### Physical Health Benefits

**Flexibility and Mobility**
One of the most immediate and well-known benefits of yoga is improved flexibility. Regular practice gradually lengthens and loosens tight muscles, increases the range of motion in joints, and reduces stiffness — particularly in the hips, hamstrings, shoulders, and spine. This enhanced mobility can improve daily functioning and reduce the risk of injury in other physical activities.

**Muscular Strength and Endurance**
Many yoga poses require supporting body weight in new ways, effectively functioning as a form of resistance training. Poses such as Plank, Chaturanga, Warrior series, and Boat Pose build functional strength in the arms, shoulders, core, legs, and back.

**Improved Balance and Coordination**
Standing and balancing poses such as Tree Pose, Warrior III, and Half Moon Pose challenge and develop proprioception — the body's sense of its own position in space. Improved balance is especially beneficial for older adults, helping to reduce fall risk.

**Pain Relief — Back Pain**
A 2020 report by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, which evaluated 10 studies involving 1,520 total participants, found that yoga improved pain and function in people with low-back pain in both the short term (1–6 months) and intermediate term (6–12 months). The effects were comparable to those of conventional exercise and massage.

**Cardiovascular Health**
Dynamic styles of yoga such as Vinyasa, Power Yoga, and Ashtanga elevate the heart rate and provide mild to moderate cardiovascular conditioning. Even gentler forms of yoga have been shown to lower resting heart rate and blood pressure, and to improve circulation.

**Respiratory Function**
Pranayama (yogic breathing) exercises strengthen the respiratory muscles, increase lung capacity, and improve overall breathing efficiency. This is beneficial for general health and may support management of respiratory conditions.

**Weight Management**
While vigorous styles of yoga can burn significant calories, even gentle yoga may support healthy weight management by reducing the stress hormone cortisol (which is associated with fat storage) and by fostering greater body awareness and mindful eating behaviors.

**Better Sleep Quality**
Research has shown that regular yoga practice may improve both how quickly people fall asleep and how deeply they sleep. This effect is attributed to a combination of the physical exertion involved, and the mental calming and stress relief that yoga specifically provides.

**Bone Health**
Weight-bearing yoga poses stimulate bone formation and may help slow bone density loss associated with aging, making yoga a potentially valuable practice for reducing osteoporosis risk.

**Digestive Health**
Twisting poses and abdominal compressions in yoga are thought to stimulate digestive organs, improve peristalsis, and relieve constipation and bloating.

**Hormonal Balance**
Regular yoga practice has been associated with regulation of cortisol and other stress hormones, which can have downstream benefits for immune function, metabolic health, and reproductive health.

### Mental Health Benefits

**Stress Reduction**
This is perhaps yoga's most widely documented psychological benefit. A 2020 review of 12 studies involving 672 total participants found beneficial effects of yoga on measures of perceived stress across all studies reviewed. A February 2024 study published in *Frontiers in Public Health* found that yoga practitioners reported significantly lower levels of stress and mental distress compared to non-practitioners.

**Reduced Anxiety**
A 2019 review of 38 studies involving 2,295 participants found that yoga had a substantial beneficial effect on anxiety symptoms. The combination of physical movement, breath regulation, and present-moment awareness appears to be particularly effective for calming the nervous system.

**Improved Mood and Depression**
Multiple studies have found yoga to reduce symptoms of depression. It is thought to work through several mechanisms, including stimulating the release of feel-good neurotransmitters (such as GABA, serotonin, and dopamine), reducing cortisol levels, and promoting a positive sense of body image and self-efficacy.

**Enhanced Cognitive Function and Brain Health**
Research has found that regular yoga practice may have a positive effect on both brain structure and function. Women who practiced yoga regularly showed more gray matter — the part of the brain that governs memory, thought, and movement — and better working memory than non-practitioners. Studies of Kundalini yoga found consistent improvements in memory performance and executive function, along with increased hippocampal volume. A 2025 report from the American Heart Association highlighted evidence that yoga may protect brain health and reduce cognitive decline.

**Better Sleep and Mental Rest**
Beyond the physical mechanisms, yoga's emphasis on mental quieting, breath awareness, and relaxation — particularly in restorative and Yin styles — helps calm the nervous system and prepares the mind for restful sleep.

**Emotional Regulation and Resilience**
A 2018 review of 14 studies involving 1,084 participants found that most studies showed evidence of yoga's beneficial effects on resilience and general mental well-being. The mindful awareness cultivated in yoga practice can improve the ability to recognize and regulate emotional responses.

**Mindfulness and Present-Moment Awareness**
Yoga is closely aligned with mindfulness — the practice of non-judgmental present-moment awareness. Regular practice has been shown to increase trait mindfulness, which is associated with a broad range of psychological benefits including reduced rumination and greater overall life satisfaction.

**Social Connection**
Group yoga classes can foster a sense of community and belonging, which is itself a significant protective factor for mental health.

## 3. Health Concerns, Contraindications & Safety Considerations

While yoga is generally safe for most healthy individuals, it is not without risks. Understanding these risks and taking appropriate precautions is essential for a safe and beneficial practice.

### Common Yoga Injuries

According to the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission, more than **34,000 yoga-related injuries** were treated in medical settings in 2019 in the United States alone, and injury rates appear to be increasing. Common yoga-related injuries include:

- **Neck and cervical spine injuries** — often from improper headstands, shoulder stands, or plow pose
- **Lower back injuries** — from forward folds, deep spinal twists, or improper alignment in backbends
- **Hamstring strains** — from overstretching in forward folds
- **Knee injuries** — including medial meniscus tears, from deep knee flexion or externally rotated hip poses
- **Hip injuries** — including hip labrum tears from deep hip-opening poses
- **Shoulder injuries** — including rotator cuff strains and labrum tears from weight-bearing arm poses
- **Wrist injuries** — from prolonged weight-bearing in Plank, Chaturanga, and arm balances
- **Compression fractures** — in the spine, particularly in older adults with osteoporosis

Research also identifies the most commonly implicated poses in acute injuries as **hand stands, headstands, and shoulder stands** (accounting for 29.4% of reported acute adverse events). Spinal flexion and extension poses are noted as particularly high-risk positions for spinal injuries.

### Specific Health Conditions Requiring Caution

**Osteoporosis / Osteopenia**
Styles of yoga that emphasize repeated forward flexion or forward bends place significant load on the vertebrae and may increase fracture risk. People with osteoporosis or low bone density should seek medical guidance and work with a qualified instructor to modify their practice. Weight-bearing poses in a modified form may actually benefit bone density, but must be approached carefully.

**Glaucoma**
Inverted poses such as headstand, shoulder stand, and downward-facing dog increase intraocular (eye) pressure, which can be harmful for people with glaucoma. Those with this condition should avoid all inversions or consult their ophthalmologist before practicing.

**High Blood Pressure (Hypertension)**
Inversions and poses that involve holding the breath (breath retention) can temporarily elevate blood pressure. People with uncontrolled hypertension should avoid these postures and focus on gentle, restorative practices with free breathing.

**Herniated or Bulging Discs**
Certain forward folds, deep twists, and backbends can exacerbate disc problems in the spine. Those with disc issues should work with a physiotherapist or specialized yoga therapist to identify safe modifications.

**Knee and Hip Problems**
Poses involving deep knee flexion (such as Hero Pose, Lotus, and deep squats) or extreme hip external rotation can aggravate existing knee or hip conditions. Modifications with props and avoiding the extremes of range of motion are advisable.

**Pregnancy**
Yoga can be safely practiced during pregnancy with appropriate modifications. During the **first trimester**, poses involving deep twists and strong abdominal contractions should be avoided. During the **second and third trimesters**, lying flat on the back for extended periods, deep belly compressions, and strong inversions should be avoided. Prenatal yoga classes led by trained instructors are recommended.

**Recent Surgery or Fractures**
Yoga should generally be avoided or significantly modified following any surgery or fracture until cleared by a medical professional.

**Cardiovascular Conditions**
People with serious heart conditions should consult their cardiologist before beginning a yoga practice. Hot yoga (Bikram) is particularly contraindicated for those with cardiovascular disease, as the combination of heat and exertion places significant demands on the heart.

**Anxiety Disorders**
Some breathing techniques, particularly rapid breathing exercises like Kapalabhati, or extended breath retention, may trigger anxiety or panic attacks in individuals with severe anxiety disorders. A slow, gentle approach with focus on calming breathwork is recommended.

**Asthma and Respiratory Conditions**
Some yoga poses and breathing techniques may trigger asthma flare-ups in susceptible individuals. Yoga should be adapted to avoid practices that strain the respiratory system, and individuals should always have their rescue inhaler accessible.

### General Safety Recommendations

- **Consult your doctor** before beginning yoga if you have any existing medical conditions, are pregnant, have recently had surgery, or are recovering from injury.
- **Start with beginner-level classes** and avoid attempting advanced postures (headstand, full lotus, deep backbends) before your body is adequately prepared.
- **Practice under qualified instruction** — the risk of chronic adverse effects is significantly higher in individuals who practice only via self-study without supervision.
- **Warm up** before practice to prepare the muscles and joints.
- **Listen to your body** — pain is a signal to stop, not push through. Distinguish between the discomfort of appropriate effort and the pain of potential injury.
- **Avoid overheating** — stay hydrated, especially in hot yoga styles.
- **Use props** (blocks, straps, bolsters, blankets) to support your body and avoid straining to reach full expressions of poses before your body is ready.
- **Inform your instructor** about any health conditions or injuries before class.
- **Avoid the "no pain, no gain" mentality** — yoga is meant to be a mindful, gradual practice, not a competition.
- **Beginners should specifically avoid** advanced breathing techniques such as Kapalabhati until they have built a solid foundational practice.

## 4. Yoga Asanas: A Comprehensive Guide

The following section details 45 of the most commonly practiced yoga asanas, organized by category. Each entry includes a description of benefits, guidance on who should avoid the pose, and step-by-step instructions.

---

### Standing Poses

---

#### 1. Mountain Pose — *Tadasana*

**Description:** The foundational standing pose of yoga. Though it appears simple, Tadasana cultivates body awareness and teaches correct postural alignment.

**Benefits:**
- Improves posture and body alignment
- Strengthens the legs, ankles, and feet
- Develops a sense of grounding and stability
- Improves focus and concentration
- Serves as the foundation for all standing poses

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Those with severe low blood pressure (may cause dizziness when standing still)
- People with headaches or insomnia (use a modified version)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Stand with your feet together or hip-width apart, big toes touching or parallel.
2. Press all four corners of each foot evenly into the floor.
3. Engage your thigh muscles and lift your kneecaps, without locking your knees.
4. Lengthen your tailbone toward the floor, engaging your lower abdominal muscles gently.
5. Broaden your collarbones and let your shoulder blades slide down your back.
6. Extend your arms alongside your body with palms facing forward.
7. Elongate your neck and balance your head directly over your pelvis.
8. Hold for 5–10 breaths, breathing steadily and deeply.

---

#### 2. Standing Forward Fold — *Uttanasana*

**Description:** A deep forward fold that stretches the entire back body, from the heels to the crown of the head.

**Benefits:**
- Stretches the hamstrings, calves, and hips
- Releases tension in the spine and neck
- Calms the nervous system and mild anxiety
- Stimulates digestion
- May help relieve headaches and mild fatigue

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with herniated discs or lower back injuries (use a significant bend in the knees)
- Those with sciatica (approach cautiously)
- People with glaucoma or high blood pressure (avoid full inversion; keep head above heart level)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Mountain Pose (Tadasana).
2. Inhale and reach your arms overhead, lengthening your spine.
3. Exhale and hinge forward from your hips (not your waist), leading with your chest.
4. Allow your hands to rest on the floor, on blocks, or on your shins — wherever they can reach without rounding the spine excessively.
5. Let the crown of your head hang toward the floor and release the neck completely.
6. On each inhale, lengthen the spine slightly; on each exhale, fold a little deeper.
7. Bend your knees as much as needed to maintain length in the spine.
8. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then slowly roll up to standing on an inhale.

---

#### 3. Chair Pose — *Utkatasana*

**Description:** A powerful standing pose that mimics the action of sitting in an imaginary chair, building strength and heat throughout the body.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the thighs, glutes, calves, and ankles
- Tones the core and back muscles
- Stimulates the heart and circulatory system
- Improves balance and proprioception
- Builds mental determination and endurance

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with knee injuries or chronic knee pain
- Those with low blood pressure (may be aggravated by the intense effort)
- People with insomnia (stimulating effect)
- Those with lower back problems (modify by reducing the depth of the squat)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Mountain Pose with feet hip-width apart.
2. Inhale and sweep your arms overhead, alongside your ears, palms facing each other.
3. Exhale and bend your knees, sinking your hips down and back as if sitting onto a chair.
4. Aim to bring your thighs as parallel to the floor as your flexibility allows.
5. Keep your knees tracking over your second toes and avoid letting them collapse inward.
6. Draw your lower belly gently in to protect your lower back.
7. Shift your weight into your heels and keep your chest lifting.
8. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then inhale and straighten your legs to release.

---

#### 4. Warrior I — *Virabhadrasana I*

**Description:** A heroic standing pose that builds strength, focus, and determination. Named after the mythological warrior Virabhadra.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the legs, hips, back, shoulders, and arms
- Opens the chest, hips, and hip flexors
- Improves balance and focus
- Energizes the body and builds endurance
- Stretches the front of the back leg's hip (iliopsoas)

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with high blood pressure (arms overhead may elevate blood pressure)
- Those with shoulder problems (modify by keeping hands at the heart or on hips)
- People with hip or knee injuries (reduce the depth of the lunge)
- Those with spinal stenosis (avoid deep lumbar arching)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Mountain Pose. Step your left foot back approximately 3–4 feet.
2. Turn your left foot out to a 45-degree angle and press the outer edge firmly into the floor.
3. Ensure your right knee is directly above your right ankle when you bend it to approximately 90 degrees.
4. Square your hips toward the front of the mat as much as possible.
5. On an inhale, sweep your arms overhead with palms facing each other (or interlace fingers and point index fingers up).
6. Draw your shoulder blades down your back, away from your ears.
7. Gaze forward or slightly upward.
8. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then step forward and repeat on the other side.

---

#### 5. Warrior II — *Virabhadrasana II*

**Description:** A wide-stance standing pose emphasizing hip opening and lateral strength, with arms extending in opposite directions.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the legs, ankles, and core
- Opens the hips and groin
- Stretches the inner thighs, chest, and shoulders
- Improves stamina and concentration
- Stimulates the abdominal organs

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with hip osteoporosis (extreme hip external rotation may be contraindicated)
- Those with neck problems (keep the gaze forward rather than over the front hand)
- People with chronic knee pain (reduce the depth of the bend)
- Those with diarrhea

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Step your feet approximately 3.5–4 feet apart.
2. Turn your right foot out 90 degrees and your left foot in slightly (about 15 degrees).
3. Align your right heel with the arch of your left foot.
4. Exhale and bend your right knee to approximately 90 degrees, stacking it over your right ankle.
5. Keep your torso directly upright over your hips — do not lean over the front leg.
6. Extend your arms out to the sides, parallel to the floor, palms down.
7. Turn your head to gaze over your right (front) hand.
8. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then straighten the front knee and repeat on the other side.

---

#### 6. Warrior III — *Virabhadrasana III*

**Description:** A single-leg balancing pose that challenges strength, coordination, and concentration simultaneously.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the entire standing leg, including the ankle, knee, and hip
- Tones the core, back, and glutes
- Improves balance and proprioception
- Enhances focus and mental steadiness
- Stretches the hamstrings and hip of the extended leg

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with balance disorders or vestibular issues (use a wall for support)
- Those with high blood pressure
- People with leg or hip injuries
- Those who are fatigued or unwell

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Warrior I with the right foot forward.
2. Shift your weight onto your right foot and slowly begin to lift your left leg behind you.
3. Simultaneously, lower your torso forward until it becomes parallel to the floor.
4. Extend your arms forward alongside your ears, or keep them along your sides or at the hips for more stability.
5. Your body should form a "T" shape — torso and lifted leg in one long diagonal line from heel to fingertips.
6. Flex your lifted foot and keep your hips squared toward the floor.
7. Fix your gaze at a point on the floor to help with balance.
8. Hold for 3–5 breaths, then slowly release. Repeat on the other side.

---

#### 7. Triangle Pose — *Trikonasana*

**Description:** A foundational standing pose that combines lateral extension with hip opening, stretching the entire side of the body.

**Benefits:**
- Stretches the hamstrings, hips, groin, and side body
- Strengthens the legs, knees, and ankles
- Opens the chest and shoulders
- Stimulates the abdominal organs and digestion
- Helps relieve stress and mild anxiety

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with low blood pressure
- Those with neck problems (look down instead of up)
- People with headaches
- Those with diarrhea

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Stand with your feet approximately 3.5–4 feet apart.
2. Turn your right foot out 90 degrees and your left foot in slightly.
3. With arms extended to the sides at shoulder height, reach your right arm out over your right leg as far as you can.
4. Lower your right hand to your right shin, ankle, or the floor outside your right foot — wherever it reaches without straining.
5. Extend your left arm straight up toward the ceiling and turn your head to look up at your left hand (or down if that strains your neck).
6. Keep both legs straight and active, with your weight distributed evenly between both feet.
7. Hold for 5–8 breaths, then rise up on an inhale and repeat on the left side.

---

#### 8. Half Moon Pose — *Ardha Chandrasana*

**Description:** An advanced balancing pose combining the lateral extension of Triangle Pose with the balance challenge of Warrior III.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the legs, ankles, and core
- Improves balance and spatial awareness
- Opens the chest and hips
- Stimulates the abdominal organs
- Builds coordination and focus

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with headaches, low blood pressure, or diarrhea
- Those with cervical neck problems (keep the gaze forward or down)
- People with knee or ankle injuries
- Beginners without adequate balance (use a wall or a block for support)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Triangle Pose with the right foot forward.
2. Bend your right knee slightly and shift your weight onto your right foot.
3. Place your right hand on a block or the floor about a foot in front of your right foot.
4. As you inhale, lift your left leg up until it is parallel to the floor, straightening both legs.
5. Open your hips so your left hip stacks above your right hip.
6. Extend your left arm straight up toward the ceiling.
7. Turn your head to look up at your left hand if comfortable, or keep your gaze forward.
8. Hold for 3–5 breaths, then slowly release and repeat on the other side.

---

#### 9. Tree Pose — *Vrksasana*

**Description:** A classic single-leg balancing pose that develops steadiness, concentration, and leg strength.

**Benefits:**
- Improves balance and coordination
- Strengthens the standing leg, ankle, and foot
- Opens the hip of the bent leg
- Builds focus and mental clarity
- Stretches the inner thigh and groin

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Those with severe balance disorders (practice near a wall)
- People with low blood pressure
- People with hip injuries affecting external rotation

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Mountain Pose with feet hip-width apart.
2. Shift your weight onto your left foot and lift your right foot off the floor.
3. Turn your right knee out to the side and place the sole of your right foot either on the inner calf or inner thigh (avoid placing it directly on the knee joint).
4. Bring your hands to prayer position at your chest, or extend your arms overhead.
5. Fix your gaze on a still point in front of you (a *drishti*) to help maintain balance.
6. Stand tall, pressing the sole of your foot into your standing leg.
7. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then release. Repeat on the other side.

---

#### 10. Eagle Pose — *Garudasana*

**Description:** A challenging balancing pose involving wrapping the arms and legs that deeply opens the shoulders, upper back, and outer hips.

**Benefits:**
- Stretches the shoulders, upper back, and outer hips
- Strengthens the ankles and legs
- Improves balance and concentration
- Relieves tension in the shoulders and neck
- Improves circulation to the joints

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with knee injuries (do not wrap the legs; instead, cross at the knee only)
- Those with shoulder injuries
- Pregnant women (balance may be compromised)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Mountain Pose.
2. Bend your knees slightly and shift your weight onto your left foot.
3. Cross your right thigh over your left thigh, then wrap your right foot around your left calf if possible.
4. Extend your arms forward at shoulder height, bend your elbows, and cross your left arm over your right.
5. Wrap your forearms around each other so your palms face each other (or toward each other).
6. Lift your elbows to shoulder height and move your forearms away from your face.
7. Hold for 5–8 breaths, then unwind and repeat on the opposite side.

---

#### 11. Dancer Pose — *Natarajasana*

**Description:** A graceful, advanced balancing backbend pose symbolizing the Hindu god Shiva Nataraja (the cosmic dancer).

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the standing leg, ankle, and core
- Opens the chest, shoulders, and hip flexors
- Develops balance, coordination, and grace
- Stimulates energy and focus
- Stretches the entire front of the body

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with low blood pressure
- Those with back, shoulder, or knee injuries
- Beginners (a modified version against a wall is recommended)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Mountain Pose. Shift your weight onto your left foot.
2. Bend your right knee and reach back with your right hand to hold the inside of your right ankle or foot.
3. Find your balance and then, on an inhale, begin to kick your right foot back and up into your hand.
4. Simultaneously tilt your torso slightly forward and extend your left arm forward for balance.
5. Continue pressing your foot back into your hand, opening the chest and lifting through the raised leg.
6. Hold for 3–5 breaths. Release slowly and repeat on the other side.

---

#### 12. Garland Pose — *Malasana*

**Description:** A deep squat pose that is natural to many cultures worldwide and offers profound benefits for the hips and lower body.

**Benefits:**
- Opens the hips, groin, and inner thighs
- Stretches the ankles and lower back
- Tones the abdominal organs and supports digestion
- Strengthens the lower body
- Activates the root chakra (Muladhara) in yogic philosophy

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with knee injuries
- Those with significant ankle stiffness (use a rolled blanket under the heels for support)
- People with lower back problems (approach cautiously)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Stand with your feet slightly wider than hip-width apart and turn your toes out slightly.
2. Bend your knees and lower your hips down into a squat, bringing your hips below your knees if possible.
3. Keep your heels on the floor (or support them with a folded blanket if needed).
4. Bring your palms together in prayer position and press your elbows against the inner sides of your knees, gently widening the knees.
5. Lengthen your spine and lift your chest.
6. Hold for 5–10 breaths.

### Seated and Floor Poses

---

#### 13. Staff Pose — *Dandasana*

**Description:** The foundational seated pose of yoga, establishing correct spinal alignment for all seated postures.

**Benefits:**
- Improves posture and spinal alignment
- Strengthens the back muscles
- Stretches the hamstrings
- Tones the abdominal muscles

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Those with tight hamstrings preventing upright seated posture (sit on a folded blanket)
- People with lower back injuries

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Sit on the floor with your legs extended straight in front of you.
2. Place your palms flat on the floor beside your hips.
3. Press through your heels and flex your feet, engaging your leg muscles.
4. Press your sitting bones into the floor and sit up as tall as possible.
5. Roll your shoulder blades back and down, lifting your chest.
6. Hold for 5–10 breaths.

---

#### 14. Seated Forward Fold — *Paschimottanasana*

**Description:** A deep forward fold performed from a seated position, offering an intense stretch of the entire back body.

**Benefits:**
- Deeply stretches the hamstrings, calves, and lower back
- Calms the nervous system and reduces stress
- Stimulates the liver, kidneys, and digestive organs
- Helps relieve mild depression and fatigue
- Improves digestion

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with herniated discs or severe lower back injuries (bend the knees generously)
- Those with hamstring injuries
- Pregnant women (avoid compressing the belly)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Staff Pose (Dandasana).
2. Inhale and lengthen your spine, reaching your arms overhead.
3. As you exhale, hinge forward from your hips — not your waist — leading with your chest.
4. Reach your hands toward your feet, shins, or use a strap around the soles of your feet.
5. Keep a long spine; avoid rounding your back to reach further.
6. With each inhale, lengthen the spine; with each exhale, fold a little deeper.
7. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then inhale and slowly sit back up.

---

#### 15. Bound Angle Pose — *Baddha Konasana*

**Description:** Also known as "Butterfly Pose," this is a classic hip-opening pose that is accessible for most levels of practitioners.

**Benefits:**
- Opens the inner thighs, groin, and hips
- Stretches the knees and ankles
- Stimulates the abdominal organs, bladder, and kidneys
- Helps relieve mild depression, anxiety, and fatigue
- Beneficial during menstruation and menopause

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with inner knee injuries
- Those with groin injuries
- Avoid forward folding if you have a herniated disc

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Sit in Staff Pose and bend your knees, drawing the soles of your feet together.
2. Let your knees fall out to the sides toward the floor.
3. Hold your ankles or feet with both hands.
4. Sit up straight, lengthening your spine.
5. To deepen the pose, gently press your thighs toward the floor with your hands or elbows.
6. Optionally, fold forward with a straight spine for a deeper hip and groin stretch.
7. Hold for 1–3 minutes.

---

#### 16. Wide-Angle Seated Forward Fold — *Upavistha Konasana*

**Description:** A seated straddle forward fold that opens the inner thighs and lengthens the spine.

**Benefits:**
- Deeply stretches the inner thighs, hamstrings, and groin
- Opens the hips
- Stimulates the abdominal organs
- Calms the mind

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with groin or hamstring injuries
- Those with herniated discs (avoid the forward fold component)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Sit on the floor and open your legs wide into a V-shape.
2. Flex your feet and press your heels into the floor.
3. Inhale, sit up tall and lengthen your spine.
4. Exhale, hinge forward from your hips, walking your hands out in front of you.
5. Keep your spine long rather than rounding.
6. Hold for 5–10 breaths.

---

#### 17. Seated Spinal Twist — *Ardha Matsyendrasana*

**Description:** A classic seated twist that detoxifies the body by stimulating the digestive organs and releases tension from the spine.

**Benefits:**
- Stretches the spine, shoulders, and hips
- Stimulates the liver and kidneys
- Improves digestion and relieves mild back pain
- Energizes the spine
- Opens the chest and shoulders

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Pregnant women (avoid deep abdominal twists)
- People with herniated discs or recent spinal surgery
- Those with severe back problems (use a gentler twist variation)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Sit in Staff Pose. Bend your right knee and place your right foot on the floor outside your left thigh.
2. Keep your left leg straight, or bend it so your left foot rests near your right hip.
3. Inhale and lengthen your spine upward.
4. Exhale and twist your torso to the right, placing your left elbow on the outside of your right knee.
5. Place your right hand on the floor behind your right hip.
6. Inhale to lengthen, exhale to deepen the twist.
7. Hold for 5–8 breaths, then release and repeat on the other side.

#### 18. Boat Pose — *Navasana*

**Description:** A challenging core-strengthening pose that requires balance, abdominal strength, and concentration.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the core, hip flexors, and spine
- Stimulates the kidneys, thyroid, intestines, and prostate
- Improves balance and digestion
- Tones the abdomen
- Builds concentration

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with neck injuries (use a strap behind the feet to avoid straining the neck)
- Those with low blood pressure
- Pregnant women
- People with diarrhea or severe asthma

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Sit in Staff Pose.
2. Bend your knees and place your feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart.
3. Place your hands behind your knees and lean back slightly until you feel your core engage.
4. Lift your feet off the floor so your shins become parallel to the floor.
5. Straighten your legs to form a "V" shape if possible (or keep knees bent for a modified version).
6. Extend your arms alongside your legs, parallel to the floor, palms up.
7. Lift your chest and lengthen your spine.
8. Hold for 5–10 breaths.

---

#### 19. Child's Pose — *Balasana*

**Description:** A gentle resting pose that provides a sense of security, deep relaxation, and a mild stretch for the back and hips. Used frequently as a rest between challenging poses.

**Benefits:**
- Gently stretches the lower back, hips, thighs, and ankles
- Calms the nervous system and relieves stress
- Releases tension in the shoulders and neck
- Offers a sense of introspection and calm
- Relieves mild back and neck pain

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Pregnant women (use a wide-knee version or avoid entirely in later stages)
- People with knee injuries (place a blanket between the thighs and calves for support)
- Those with ankle problems

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin on all fours (tabletop position).
2. Bring your big toes together and take your knees wide (wide-legged version) or keep them together (traditional version).
3. Exhale and lower your hips back toward your heels.
4. Extend your arms forward on the floor or rest them alongside your body.
5. Let your forehead rest on the floor or on a block or folded blanket.
6. Release your weight downward and breathe deeply into your back.
7. Hold for 1–3 minutes or as long as desired.

#### 20. Happy Baby Pose — *Ananda Balasana*

**Description:** A playful supine pose that releases the hips and lower back, often used as a gentle cooldown posture.

**Benefits:**
- Gently opens the hips and groin
- Stretches the inner thighs and lower back
- Calms the nervous system
- Relieves stress and fatigue
- Gently decompresses the spine

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Pregnant women (avoid in later trimesters)
- People with knee or ankle injuries
- Those with neck injuries (keep the head on the floor)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie on your back and exhale, drawing your knees toward your belly.
2. Inhale and grip the outer edges of your feet with your hands (or use a strap around the soles of the feet if you can't reach).
3. Open your knees, bringing them toward your armpits.
4. Align each ankle directly over the corresponding knee.
5. Flex your feet and gently press them up into your hands while drawing them down.
6. Keep your lower back pressed into the floor.
7. Hold for 1–2 minutes and breathe deeply.

### Supine and Prone Poses

---

#### 21. Cat-Cow Pose — *Marjariasana-Bitilasana*

**Description:** A flowing movement between two poses that gently massages the spine and is an excellent warm-up for the back.

**Benefits:**
- Increases spinal flexibility and mobility
- Massages the spine and organs of the belly
- Strengthens and stretches the back and neck
- Improves posture and balance
- Provides emotional release and stress relief

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with neck injuries (keep the neck neutral without lifting or dropping the head)
- Those with wrist injuries (use fists or forearms on the floor)
- Individuals with knee problems (place a folded blanket under the knees)
- Pregnant women in the first trimester should approach cautiously; generally Cat-Cow is helpful in pregnancy but check with a healthcare provider

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin on all fours in a tabletop position — wrists under shoulders, knees under hips.
2. **Cow Pose (Bitilasana):** Inhale, drop your belly toward the floor, lift your chest and tailbone toward the ceiling, and look gently upward.
3. **Cat Pose (Marjariasana):** Exhale, round your spine toward the ceiling, tuck your chin to your chest and your tailbone under.
4. Continue flowing between these two movements, following the rhythm of your breath.
5. Repeat for 5–10 breath cycles.

---

#### 22. Cobra Pose — *Bhujangasana*

**Description:** A gentle backbend performed lying face down that strengthens the spine and opens the chest.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the spine and back muscles
- Stretches the chest, shoulders, and abdomen
- Opens the lungs and improves breathing
- Stimulates the abdominal organs
- Relieves mild fatigue and stress

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Pregnant women
- People with back injuries, herniated discs, or carpal tunnel syndrome
- Those with headaches

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie face down with your legs extended behind you, feet hip-width apart.
2. Place your palms flat on the floor under your shoulders, fingers spread wide.
3. Press the tops of your feet, thighs, and pubic bone into the floor.
4. Inhale and begin to straighten your arms, lifting your chest off the floor.
5. Keep a slight bend in your elbows (avoid locking them).
6. Roll your shoulders back and down, away from your ears, and lift your sternum.
7. Distribute the backbend evenly along your entire spine — do not compress only the lower back.
8. Hold for 5–8 breaths, then exhale and lower down.

---

#### 23. Locust Pose — *Shalabhasana*

**Description:** A prone backbend that builds strength along the entire posterior chain of the body.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the muscles of the back, glutes, and legs
- Stretches the chest, shoulders, and abdomen
- Improves posture and spinal extension
- Stimulates the digestive organs
- Helps relieve mild back pain

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Pregnant women
- People with herniated discs or severe lower back injuries
- Those with recent abdominal surgery

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie face down with your arms alongside your body, palms up. Rest your forehead on the floor.
2. On an inhale, simultaneously lift your head, chest, arms, and legs off the floor.
3. Keep your gaze forward and slightly down to keep the neck long.
4. Engage your gluteal muscles and thighs.
5. Reach your arms back, fingers stretching toward your feet.
6. Hold for 5–8 breaths, then exhale and slowly lower down. Rest for a breath cycle, then repeat 2–3 times.

---

#### 24. Upward Facing Dog — *Urdhva Mukha Svanasana*

**Description:** A deeper backbend than Cobra, lifting the thighs and hips off the floor as well. A key pose in the Sun Salutation sequence.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the spine, arms, and wrists
- Stretches the chest, abdomen, and hip flexors
- Improves posture
- Invigorates the body
- Stimulates the abdominal organs

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Pregnant women
- People with back injuries
- Those with carpal tunnel syndrome or wrist pain
- People with headaches

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in the same starting position as Cobra Pose.
2. On an inhale, press firmly through your palms and straighten your arms fully.
3. Lift your torso, hips, and thighs completely off the floor. Only the tops of your feet and your palms touch the floor.
4. Roll your shoulders back and down, and lift through your sternum.
5. Engage your leg muscles and keep your thighs firm.
6. Hold for 3–5 breaths, then either push back to Downward Facing Dog or lower to the floor.

---

#### 25. Bridge Pose — *Setu Bandha Sarvangasana*

**Description:** A supine backbend that strengthens the back, glutes, and legs while opening the chest and hip flexors.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the back, glutes, and hamstrings
- Opens the chest, shoulders, and hip flexors
- Calms the nervous system and reduces anxiety
- Stimulates the abdominal organs and lungs
- Helps relieve headaches, mild depression, and fatigue

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with neck injuries (never turn the head while in the pose)
- Those with severe back problems
- Pregnant women in later trimesters

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart. Place your feet directly under your knees.
2. Rest your arms alongside your body, palms down.
3. Press your feet firmly into the floor and, on an inhale, lift your hips toward the ceiling.
4. Roll your shoulders under your body and interlace your fingers below your back (or keep palms pressing into the floor).
5. Keep your thighs parallel and knees directly over your ankles.
6. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then exhale and slowly lower your spine down vertebra by vertebra.

---

#### 26. Wheel Pose / Upward Bow — *Urdhva Dhanurasana*

**Description:** A full backbend and advanced pose that powerfully opens the entire front of the body.

**Benefits:**
- Powerfully stretches the chest, abdomen, and hip flexors
- Strengthens the arms, wrists, spine, and legs
- Stimulates the thyroid and pituitary glands
- Increases energy and counters depression
- Improves spinal flexibility

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Beginners (work up to this gradually with Bridge and Cobra poses first)
- People with back injuries, herniated discs, or spinal stenosis
- Those with wrist, elbow, or shoulder injuries
- People with carpal tunnel syndrome
- Those with high blood pressure, heart conditions, or headaches
- Pregnant women

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart.
2. Place your palms on the floor beside your ears, fingers pointing toward your shoulders.
3. Press evenly through your palms and feet and, on an inhale, lift your hips and torso off the floor.
4. Press up onto the crown of your head briefly, then straighten your arms fully.
5. Roll your shoulders back and open the chest fully.
6. Keep your feet parallel and knees pointed forward.
7. Hold for 3–5 breaths, then tuck your chin and lower down slowly and carefully.

---

#### 27. Reclining Twist — *Supta Matsyendrasana*

**Description:** A gentle supine spinal twist that releases the lower back and is an excellent pose for relaxation and recovery.

**Benefits:**
- Gently releases the lower back and spine
- Stretches the outer hips and glutes (piriformis)
- Stimulates the digestive organs
- Calms the nervous system
- Relieves mild back pain and fatigue

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with herniated discs (keep the twist very minimal)
- Pregnant women (may need a modified version)
- Those with hip injuries

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie on your back and draw both knees into your chest.
2. Extend your arms out to the sides at shoulder height, palms down.
3. Exhale and lower both knees to the right side, stacking them on top of each other.
4. Turn your head gently to the left.
5. Let gravity do the work — do not force the twist.
6. Hold for 1–3 minutes, breathing deeply.
7. Inhale and draw your knees back to center, then repeat on the left side.

---

#### 28. Legs Up the Wall — *Viparita Karani*

**Description:** A restorative gentle inversion where the legs rest against a wall, offering profound relaxation and circulatory benefits.

**Benefits:**
- Relieves tired and swollen legs and feet
- Calms the nervous system and reduces anxiety
- Alleviates mild depression, headaches, and insomnia
- Improves circulation and lymphatic drainage
- Provides gentle traction for the lower back

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with serious eye conditions such as glaucoma (any inversion increases eye pressure)
- Those with uncontrolled high blood pressure
- People with serious neck problems

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Sit sideways next to a wall with one hip touching the wall.
2. Swing your legs up the wall as you lower your torso to the floor.
3. Adjust your position so your sitting bones are as close to the wall as comfortable and your legs are vertical.
4. Extend your arms out to the sides or rest them on your belly.
5. Close your eyes and breathe slowly and deeply.
6. Hold for 5–15 minutes.
7. To come out, bend your knees, push your feet against the wall, and roll to one side before sitting up.

---

#### 29. Corpse Pose — *Savasana*

**Description:** The quintessential relaxation pose of yoga, practiced at the end of every session. Though seemingly simple, it is considered one of the most challenging poses due to the difficulty of achieving conscious total relaxation.

**Benefits:**
- Brings the body and mind to complete rest
- Integrates the benefits of all preceding poses
- Deeply calms the nervous system
- Reduces blood pressure
- Relieves stress, anxiety, and fatigue
- Promotes overall sense of well-being

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Pregnant women (lie on the left side instead of flat on the back in the second and third trimesters)
- People with significant back discomfort (place a bolster under the knees)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie flat on your back with your legs extended and slightly apart.
2. Let your feet fall open naturally.
3. Rest your arms alongside your body, slightly away from your torso, palms facing up.
4. Close your eyes and let your whole body sink into the floor.
5. Consciously release tension from every part of your body, starting from the toes and working up to your face.
6. Breathe naturally and allow your mind to become still.
7. Remain in the pose for 5–15 minutes.
8. To come out, deepen your breath, gently wiggle your fingers and toes, roll to one side, and press yourself up to a seated position.

### Inversion Poses

---

#### 30. Downward Facing Dog — *Adho Mukha Svanasana*

**Description:** One of the most recognized and widely practiced yoga poses, serving simultaneously as a resting pose, a transition, and a strengthening inversion.

**Benefits:**
- Energizes and rejuvenates the body
- Stretches the hamstrings, calves, shoulders, and spine
- Strengthens the arms, shoulders, and legs
- Calms the nervous system and relieves mild anxiety
- Improves circulation and relieves mild headaches and fatigue

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with carpal tunnel syndrome or wrist injuries (use fists or forearms)
- Those with high blood pressure (limit time in the pose)
- People with eye or ear infections
- Those with glaucoma (avoid all inversions)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin on all fours in tabletop position.
2. Tuck your toes under and, on an exhale, lift your hips up and back to form an inverted "V" shape.
3. Spread your fingers wide and press firmly through all ten fingertips and the base of each finger.
4. Straighten your arms and rotate them so that your inner elbows face each other.
5. Press your heels toward the floor (they do not need to touch the floor).
6. Release the crown of your head toward the floor and let the neck relax.
7. Hold for 5–10 breaths.

---

#### 31. Supported Shoulder Stand — *Salamba Sarvangasana*

**Description:** Often called the "queen of asanas," this major inversion offers extensive benefits for the whole body but requires careful technique and preparation.

**Benefits:**
- Stimulates the thyroid and parathyroid glands
- Calms the nervous system and relieves stress
- Stretches the shoulders and neck
- Improves circulation from the legs back to the heart
- Helps manage fatigue, insomnia, and mild depression

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with neck injuries (this is contraindicated — never attempt without expert guidance)
- Those with high blood pressure (approach very cautiously)
- People with glaucoma
- Pregnant women (after the first trimester)
- Menstruating women (inversions during menstruation are traditionally avoided in many yoga lineages)
- Beginners (learn with a qualified instructor)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie on your back and bend your knees, bringing them toward your chest.
2. Place a folded blanket under your shoulders for support (this is important to protect the cervical spine).
3. On an exhale, swing your legs up and over your head, supporting your back with your hands.
4. Walk your hands down toward your shoulders and straighten your legs vertically.
5. Keep your weight on your shoulders, upper arms, and the back of your head — not on your neck.
6. Engage your core and keep your legs together and active.
7. Hold for 30 seconds to 3 minutes, building time gradually.
8. To come down, bend your knees and slowly roll the spine back down to the floor.

---

#### 32. Plow Pose — *Halasana*

**Description:** An inversion that extends the shoulder stand, lowering the legs behind the head to provide a deep stretch of the spine and posterior body.

**Benefits:**
- Stretches the spine, shoulders, and hamstrings
- Stimulates the abdominal organs and thyroid gland
- Calms the nervous system
- Relieves stress and fatigue
- May help with insomnia and headaches

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with neck injuries (this is a significant contraindication)
- Those with diarrhea
- Pregnant women
- People with high blood pressure
- Menstruating women (traditional caution)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Supported Shoulder Stand.
2. Slowly lower your legs over your head until your feet touch or approach the floor behind you.
3. Keep your hands on your back for support, or extend your arms along the floor and interlace your fingers.
4. Avoid turning your head while in this pose.
5. Hold for 30 seconds to 2 minutes, breathing steadily.
6. To come out, slowly roll your spine back to the floor with control.

---

#### 33. Headstand — *Sirsasana*

**Description:** Called the "king of asanas," this full inversion is considered the most powerful and demanding of the classical yoga inversions.

**Benefits:**
- Builds significant strength in the shoulders, core, and upper body
- Calms the nervous system and relieves stress
- Stimulates the pituitary and pineal glands
- Improves circulation and relieves fatigue
- Develops focus, body awareness, and confidence

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Beginners (must be learned only under direct supervision)
- People with neck injuries or any cervical spine conditions (strictly contraindicated)
- Those with high blood pressure
- People with glaucoma or retinal problems
- Pregnant women
- Anyone with heart conditions, headaches, or ear infections

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. This pose must be learned with a qualified yoga teacher. The following steps are a general guide.
2. Place a folded blanket on the floor and interlace your fingers, creating a triangular base with your forearms.
3. Place the crown of your head on the floor between your hands.
4. Lift your knees off the floor and walk your feet toward your head until your hips are over your shoulders.
5. Engage your core and lift one leg, then the other, toward the ceiling.
6. Straighten your body into one vertical line, with your hips over your shoulders and shoulders over your wrists.
7. Distribute your weight evenly between your forearms — never compress the neck.
8. Practice at a wall until you are confident and stable.

### Core and Arm Balance Poses

---

#### 34. Plank Pose — *Phalakasana*

**Description:** A fundamental pose that builds core and upper body strength as well as total body alignment.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the core, shoulders, arms, and wrists
- Tones the spine and abdominal muscles
- Improves posture and body alignment
- Builds endurance and mental focus

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with wrist injuries (modify using fists or forearms)
- Those with shoulder injuries (reduce to knees-down variation)
- People with lower back pain (use the modified knee variation)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Downward Facing Dog.
2. On an inhale, shift forward until your shoulders are directly over your wrists.
3. Your body should form a straight diagonal line from the crown of your head to your heels.
4. Engage your abdominal muscles strongly and do not let your hips sag or rise.
5. Press the floor away with your palms and firm your shoulder blades against your back.
6. Hold for 5–10 breaths (or 20–60 seconds), breathing steadily.

---

#### 35. Four-Limbed Staff Pose — *Chaturanga Dandasana*

**Description:** A low plank position that is a key component of the Sun Salutation flow and requires significant upper body strength.

**Benefits:**
- Builds significant strength in the arms, wrists, and core
- Tones the abdominal muscles
- Prepares the body for more advanced arm balances

**Who Should Avoid:**
- Beginners (practice with knees on the floor until arm strength develops)
- People with wrist, shoulder, or elbow injuries
- Pregnant women

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Plank Pose.
2. Exhale and lower your body toward the floor, keeping your elbows close to your ribs.
3. Stop when your upper arms are parallel to the floor — your body hovers a few inches off the ground.
4. Keep your entire body in a straight line; do not let your hips sag or rise.
5. Hold for 1–3 breaths, then either press up to Upward Facing Dog or lower to the floor.

---

#### 36. Side Plank — *Vasisthasana*

**Description:** A challenging arm balance and side-strengthening pose that requires full-body integration.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the arms, wrists, and core
- Tones the obliques and lateral stabilizing muscles
- Improves balance and coordination
- Builds full-body strength and stability

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with wrist, elbow, or shoulder injuries (use a forearm variation)
- Those with significant core weakness (use the modified version with the lower knee on the floor)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Plank Pose.
2. Shift your weight onto your right hand and outer right foot.
3. Stack your left foot on top of your right foot.
4. Lift your left arm toward the ceiling, forming a straight line from your right hand through your left fingertips.
5. Keep your hips lifted — they should not sag.
6. Hold for 3–5 breaths, then return to plank and repeat on the other side.

---

#### 37. Crow Pose — *Bakasana*

**Description:** The most accessible arm balance pose, crow pose builds arm and core strength while challenging fear and developing focus.

**Benefits:**
- Strengthens the arms, wrists, and core
- Tones the upper back
- Improves balance and body awareness
- Builds confidence and mental focus

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with wrist, elbow, or shoulder injuries
- Those with carpal tunnel syndrome
- Pregnant women

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in a deep squat (Garland Pose).
2. Place your palms flat on the floor, shoulder-width apart, fingers spread wide.
3. Bend your elbows and lean your torso forward, placing your knees on the backs of your upper arms (as close to your armpits as possible).
4. Shift your weight forward into your hands and lift one foot off the floor, then the other.
5. Draw your heels toward your buttocks and engage your core.
6. Hold for 3–5 breaths, building up time with practice.

### Hip Opening and Stretching Poses

---

#### 38. Low Lunge — *Anjaneyasana*

**Description:** A deep hip flexor stretch that is accessible to most practitioners and is a common component of Sun Salutation sequences.

**Benefits:**
- Deeply stretches the hip flexors and quadriceps of the back leg
- Strengthens the front leg
- Opens the chest and shoulders when arms are raised
- Relieves stress and fatigue

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with knee injuries (place a blanket under the back knee)
- Those with hip flexor injuries

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Downward Facing Dog.
2. Step your right foot forward between your hands, lowering your left knee to the floor.
3. Align your right knee directly over your right ankle.
4. On an inhale, lift your torso and sweep your arms overhead.
5. Draw your shoulder blades down your back and lift your chest.
6. Sink your hips forward and down.
7. Hold for 5–8 breaths, then step back and repeat on the other side.

#### 39. Pigeon Pose — *Eka Pada Rajakapotasana*

**Description:** A powerful hip opener that targets the external rotators of the front hip and the hip flexors of the back leg.

**Benefits:**
- Deeply opens the hip flexors and outer hips
- Stretches the piriformis and glutes
- Relieves compression in the lower back
- May help relieve sciatic nerve pain
- Releases stored emotional tension held in the hips

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with knee injuries (the front knee is at risk; use a modification such as Supine Pigeon/Figure-4 instead)
- Those with hip injuries or hip replacement surgery
- People with sacroiliac joint issues

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Downward Facing Dog.
2. Bring your right knee forward and place it behind your right wrist, with your right foot angling toward your left wrist (the angle of the shin depends on your hip flexibility).
3. Extend your left leg back along the floor.
4. Square your hips toward the front of the mat.
5. Inhale and lengthen your spine. Exhale and fold forward over your right shin, resting on your forearms or fully extended arms.
6. Hold for 1–3 minutes, breathing deeply.
7. Press up, step back to Downward Facing Dog, and repeat on the other side.

#### 40. Lizard Pose — *Utthan Pristhasana*

**Description:** A deep lunge variation that intensely opens the hip flexors, groin, and inner thighs.

**Benefits:**
- Opens the hip flexors, groins, and inner thighs
- Stretches the hamstrings of the back leg
- Strengthens the legs
- Prepares the body for deeper hip-opening poses

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with hip or groin injuries
- Those with knee problems (place back knee on the floor)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin in Downward Facing Dog and step your right foot forward to the outside of your right hand.
2. Lower your back knee to the floor (or keep it lifted for a more demanding version).
3. Both hands are inside your right foot on the floor (or on blocks).
4. You may lower onto your forearms for a deeper stretch.
5. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then step back to Downward Facing Dog and repeat on the other side.

---

#### 41. Puppy Pose — *Uttana Shishosana*

**Description:** A combination of Child's Pose and Downward Facing Dog that provides a wonderful upper back and shoulder stretch.

**Benefits:**
- Stretches the spine, shoulders, upper back, and arms
- Opens the chest
- Calms the mind and relieves stress
- Counteracts the effects of hunching and desk work

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with knee injuries (place a blanket under the knees)
- Those with serious shoulder or back injuries

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Begin on all fours in tabletop position.
2. Walk your hands forward, allowing your chest to drop toward the floor.
3. Keep your hips directly over your knees and let your forehead rest on the floor.
4. Press your palms into the floor and extend your arms forward.
5. Hold for 5–10 breaths, melting your chest and chin toward the floor.

### Backbend Poses

---

#### 42. Camel Pose — *Ustrasana*

**Description:** A powerful kneeling backbend that fully opens the front of the body and counteracts the effects of forward-hunching.

**Benefits:**
- Opens the chest, shoulders, and hip flexors
- Stretches the entire front of the body
- Strengthens the back muscles
- Stimulates the nervous system and increases energy
- Improves posture

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with serious lower back injuries or spinal stenosis
- Those with neck injuries (keep the head neutral rather than dropping it back)
- People with high or low blood pressure
- Those with migraines

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Kneel on the floor with your knees hip-width apart, thighs perpendicular to the floor.
2. Place your hands on your lower back, fingers pointing down.
3. Inhale and lift your chest, drawing your shoulder blades together.
4. Begin to arch your back, leaning back gradually.
5. Reach your hands back toward your heels, grasping them if possible.
6. Keep your hips directly over your knees and do not let them sag forward.
7. Let your head drop back gently if comfortable.
8. Hold for 3–5 breaths, then bring your hands back to your lower back and slowly rise.

---

#### 43. Fish Pose — *Matsyasana*

**Description:** A gentle supine backbend that opens the chest and throat and is the traditional counter-pose to Shoulder Stand.

**Benefits:**
- Opens the chest, throat, and front of the neck
- Stretches the hip flexors and intercostals
- Stimulates the thyroid and parathyroid glands
- Improves respiratory function
- Helps relieve respiratory conditions and mild congestion

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with serious neck or back injuries
- Those with low blood pressure
- People with migraines
- Those with insomnia (stimulating effect)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Lie on your back with your legs extended and arms alongside your body.
2. Slide your hands, palms down, under your buttocks.
3. Press your forearms and elbows into the floor and on an inhale, lift your chest, arching your back.
4. Tilt your head back and gently rest the crown of your head on the floor (the weight should be on your elbows, not your head).
5. Hold for 5–10 breaths, then lower your head, press into your elbows, and lower your torso to the floor.

### Restorative and Pranayama Poses

---

#### 44. Easy Pose — *Sukhasana*

**Description:** The classic seated meditation posture, used for pranayama (breathwork), meditation, and as a general starting and resting pose.

**Benefits:**
- Calms the mind and prepares it for meditation
- Opens the hips and outer thighs
- Lengthens the spine when practiced with proper alignment
- Promotes inner calm and equanimity

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with hip or knee injuries (sit on a folded blanket or use a chair)
- Those with sacroiliac joint problems

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Sit on the floor (or a folded blanket if your hips are tight) and cross your legs, placing each foot beneath the opposite knee.
2. Place your hands on your knees, palms down (for grounding) or palms up (for receptivity).
3. Sit up tall, lengthening the crown of your head toward the ceiling.
4. Soften your face, jaw, and shoulders.
5. Close your eyes and breathe slowly and deeply.
6. Alternate which leg is in front in subsequent sessions to maintain symmetry.

---

#### 45. Reclined Butterfly / Reclined Bound Angle — *Supta Baddha Konasana*

**Description:** A restorative supine version of Bound Angle Pose, typically held for several minutes with the support of props.

**Benefits:**
- Opens the inner thighs, groin, and hips passively
- Deeply calms the nervous system
- Relieves stress, anxiety, and fatigue
- Helpful for menstrual discomfort
- Relieves mild depression

**Who Should Avoid:**
- People with hip injuries (support the thighs with rolled blankets or blocks to prevent over-stretching)
- Those with lower back problems (support the spine with a bolster)

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Sit on the floor with your knees bent and the soles of your feet together (as in Bound Angle Pose).
2. Place a bolster or rolled blankets along your spine for support.
3. Gently lie back onto the bolster or floor.
4. Rest your arms out to the sides, palms up.
5. If the inner thighs feel strained, place a folded blanket or yoga block under each thigh for support.
6. Close your eyes and breathe slowly and deeply.
7. Hold for 3–10 minutes.
